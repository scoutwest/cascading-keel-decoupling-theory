## 1. Purpose and scope

**Purpose.** Show one physically possible way Noah's Flood could have happened without Baumgardner's Catastrophic Plate Tectonics (CPT) and its "heat problem" (runaway subduction and the rapid making of today's ocean floor, which put 10²⁸-10²⁹ J into the ocean in about a year and boil it).

**CKDT's core claim in one paragraph.** Continents sit on deep, cold mantle roots ("keels"). If the dense lower part of a keel breaks away and sinks, the continent above is pulled down while the keel descends. Land goes under water without any new ocean floor being made. The energy is released 100-200 km down, where conduction needs about 10¹⁶ s to reach the surface, so the ocean never receives it (constraint H3). Failure spreads from keel to keel because each failure loads its neighbours (stress transfer), and because wet olivine under high stress flows faster by orders of magnitude (power-law creep plus shear heating).

**What CKDT is for, and what it is not for.**
- **It is a flood (submergence) mechanism.** It lowers land relative to sea level. It adds no water and makes no ocean floor.
- **It is not a continent-making or continental-drift mechanism.** CKDT covers only subsidence and destabilization. Making the continents, moving them, and everything after that is handed to DTAT (Section 5.2). Opening the Atlantic and Indian oceans (H9) and moving continents 3,800 km (H10) are therefore outside CKDT (Section 9, items O1 and O2), and the heat limits H9 and H10 still have to be met by DTAT. Any version of this model that claims CKDT also solves them is wrong by v2's results.

## 3. The heat problem and the constraints it sets

### 3.1 The problem
Moving continents and making ocean floor in about a year turns stored thermal and gravitational energy into heat. Heat leaves the ocean slowly (radiation) and rock gives up heat slowly (conduction). Both are rate problems: too fast in, too slow out.

### 3.2 Numbers (v2 Phase 1; sources in v2 `sources/README.md`, S01-S04)
| Quantity | Value |
|----------|-------|
| Ocean heat capacity | 5.5×10²⁴ J/K |
| Energy to warm ocean 15 → 100 °C / to boil it | 4.75×10²⁶ J / 3.5×10²⁷ J |
| **Adopted heat budget B** (ocean limited to 40 °C bulk, my assumption) | **1.4×10²⁶ J** (comfort tier 30 °C: 8.4×10²⁵ J) |
| Fresh hot oceanic lithosphere allowed at 3.9×10¹⁴ J/m² | 3.6×10¹¹ m² (0.10-0.12% of today's ocean floor) |
| Runaway subduction (Baumgardner) | 1×10²⁸ J = 72 B (v2 recomputed the "equivalents" to about 3×10²⁸ J) |
| Making today's ocean floor in the Flood | 1.4×10²⁹ J = about 1,000 B |
| LIPs, gross | 4×10²⁶ J = 2.9 B |
| Conduction time through thickness z (t = z²/κ, κ = 10⁻⁶ m²/s) | 1 m: 12 d; 10 m: 3.2 yr; 100 m: 320 yr; 1 km: 3×10⁴ yr; 10 km: 3×10⁶ yr; 100 km: 3×10⁸ yr |

### 3.3 Design constraints (all later sections must satisfy these)
- **H1 (area).** Fresh hot oceanic lithosphere exposed to seawater ≲ 3.6×10¹¹ m².
- **H2 (rate).** Heat into the ocean during the Flood ≲ 4.4×10¹⁸ W averaged (about 25 times sunlight).
- **H3 (location).** A heat-releasing process must dissipate deeper than about 10 km, or it counts against H1 and H2.
- **H4 (no fresh-seafloor shortcut).** Today's ocean floor cannot have been freshly made from hot mantle during the Flood.
- **H5 (water temperature).** Released water must average ≤ 159 °C (present-depth ocean) or ≤ 47 °C (shallow pre-Flood ocean). That means source depth ≲ 5 km or ≲ 1 km.
- **H6 (water budget).** Water released plus Pangea subsidence must satisfy D = max(0, (h_max + 6.9 m − f·h_P − V_r/A_Earth) / (1 − f)).
- **H7 (keel sign).** A keel mechanism must state its net buoyancy sign.
- **H8 (subsidence budget).** Keel subsidence, stretching and released water must together meet the H6 line.
- **H9 (new floor).** The model must say how the 1.64×10⁸ km² of ocean floor on continent-bearing plates arose within H1-H3.
- **H10 (motion).** The model must name the energy and weak layer that move blocks about 3,800 km, with dissipation staying deep and below melting.

## 4. The CKDT mechanism (merged and corrected version)

v1 and v2 disagree on several points (Section 8). Where they conflict, this section uses the version that survived the harder test, and says so.

### 4.1 Keels exist and are large
- Hasterok et al. (2022) gives 114 craton polygons covering 4.08×10⁷ km² (8% of Earth, 24% of continental-province area). At 150-250 km thick (Pearson et al. 2021) that is 6.1-10.2×10⁹ km³. *(v2 5.5; checked against dataset)*
- Seismic tomography and mantle xenoliths show cold, depleted peridotite roots 150-300+ km deep. *(v1 13.0; literature, not re-verified)*

### 4.2 How much subsidence is needed: about 1 km, not 3-9 km
v1 assumed 3,000-9,000 m of subsidence was needed. **v2's geometry replaces that.** With Pangea covering 41.3% of the Earth (2.105×10⁸ km²) and mean elevation 200 m, covering a 610 m peak by 15 cubits (6.86 m) needs **D ≈ 910 m** of uniform subsidence (839-980 m for 100-300 m mean elevation), with ocean floor fixed. Sea level elsewhere falls 220-360 m. *(v2 3.4; arithmetic checked in v2)*

This lowers CKDT's burden by a factor of 3-10 and is the main reason the model is more credible than v1's.

### 4.3 The buoyancy sign problem, and the repair (CKDT-II)
**Problem (v2 5.5, obstacle 27).** Removing a keel of thickness t that is lighter than the asthenosphere by fraction ε lowers the surface by ε·t. If the keel is denser, removal raises the surface.

| Chemical lightness | Net (with ≈2.3% thermal density excess) | Surface change if removed (t = 200 km) |
|--------------------|------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------|
| 0.5% | +1.8% (dense) | +3.6 km (uplift) |
| 1.0% | +1.3% | +2.6 km |
| 1.5% | +0.8% | +1.6 km |
| 2.0% | +0.3% | +0.6 km |
| 2.5% | −0.2% (light) | −0.4 km (subsidence) |

Published views conflict: isopycnic (neutral), "neutral or positive", and a 2022 study arguing net negative buoyancy (dense). So v1's original CKDT contains a contradiction: **a keel light enough to sink the continent when it leaves is too light to sink away on its own, and a keel dense enough to sink away raises the continent when it is gone.** v1's table of 1,000-6,000 m subsidence used the chemical deficit alone and ignored the thermal excess. That table is **withdrawn**.

**Repair: CKDT-II (dense keel drip).** The sinking part is dense (net ε about 0.3-1%). While it is descending, it pulls the surface down by dynamic subsidence, about −C·ε·t with C between 0.1 and 1 (0.03-2 km). When it has detached, the surface rebounds by about the same amount. This gives flooding at the start, and uplift later, which matches the overshoot-and-rebound target in P6. C is unverified (Section 9, item O5; Phase B).

**Phase A update.** Sourced chemical depletion averages 1.1-1.5% (maximum 1.7-2.5%, Kaban et al. 2003). Against a thermal excess of about 2.3% that gives a net-dense keel (+0.8 to +1.2%). The isopycnic view gives net zero. No source supports a net-light keel, so CKDT-II is the only version consistent with the data (Section 14.2).

**Consequence.** CKDT-II only acts under the 24% of continental area that has keels. Non-keel areas need another subsidence source: stretching (4.5) or released water (5.1).

### 4.4 Making fast failure physical: wet power-law creep
**Base problem.** Newtonian delamination takes millions of years (v1: τ ≈ η/(Δρ·g·h), η = 10²³ Pa·s gives 16 My; v2: 10¹⁹ Pa·s and a 100 km block with 30 kg/m³ excess takes 32,000 years to sink its own size).

**v2's result, using Hirth & Kohlstedt (2000) olivine flow laws** (v2 implementation reproduces the paper's footnote example: 2.47×10⁻¹² vs 2.5×10⁻¹² /s):
| Condition (30 kg/m³ excess, sink one block size) | Time |
|-----------|------|
| Wet (about 1,000 H/10⁶ Si, about 60 ppm water), 1,650 K, tens of MPa | about 66 days |
| Wet, 1,500 K | about 10 years |
| Wet, 100 kg/m³, 1,650 K | about 1 day |
| Dry, 1,650 K | 150 years |
| Dry, 1,500 K | 14,000 years |

So the gap between "millions of years" and "days" is largely a **water and stress** question, not a violation of physics. Thermal runaway from shear heating (+400 K) finishes under 150 days in 7 of 32 tested cases, nearly all wet, needing at least 50-100 MPa and 1,500 K or more. The keel interior is colder than that (see 7.3).

**v1's two levers, kept in simplified form.**
1. Shear heating concentrated in a 50-100 km zone gives ΔT of 120-240 K and 1.5-3 orders of viscosity reduction. Narrower zones (10 km) give 1,200 K, which is unphysical. *(v1 13.3; not rederived)*
2. Power-law amplification (strain rate ∝ σ^3.5) from stress transfer. A 5× stress boost gives about 3 orders of magnitude. *(v1 13.4)*
Together v1 reached 4-6 orders, enough to close the v1 gap. v2's wet-olivine result is a more direct route and is preferred for the model. Both depend on **water in the keel base**. Phase A found measured keel-base water is often lower than the wet case, and the fastest case is hotter than the keel base (Section 14.2). See Section 9, item O3.

### 4.5 Second subsidence source: stretching (no keel physics needed)
McKenzie-type initial subsidence (textbook form; v2's code applied the factor (1 − αT_m) twice, which overstated results by 4.6%, corrected in Phase A): β = 1.2 → 722 m; β = 1.5 → 1,445 m; β = 2 → 2,167 m (water-loaded; 35 km crust, 125 km lithosphere). The dataset's transitional crust (136 provinces, 3.8×10⁷ km², 18% of continental plus transitional) has mean elevation −1,360 m and is 92.6% submerged, a fall of about 1,560 m from a +200 m start, consistent with β of about 1.5-1.6 (corrected values). Zealandia (4.9×10⁶ km², about 94% submerged; Mortimer et al. 2017) is the real example of stretched, permanently submerged continent. Stretching energy (3.1×10²⁷ J if all transitional crust) stays deep and passes H3, unless decompression melt erupts (then it counts against H1).

### 4.6 Trigger and cascade
- **Cascade (stress transfer).** One failure loads its neighbours. v1 used the median earthquake stress drop (4 MPa) against low ambient shear-zone stress (1 MPa) for a 5× multiplier. *(v1 13.5; checked against literature, but see O6.)*
- **Trigger.** **Unresolved.** Candidates checked and not working as proposed: eclogitization (wrong material: it acts on mafic crust, not depleted peridotite, and needs prior thickening); plume arrival (10-30 My by default, 540-27,000 yr at the extreme edge, covers arrival only); Moho peeling (faster baseline, still needs an external trigger; kept as an alternative geometry, Section 4.7). See Section 9, item O4.
- **Stalling risk.** Real numerical models show delamination can stall when cooling of inflowing asthenosphere outpaces instability growth (Colorado Plateau example). "Self-sustaining" has not been shown. See O7.

### 4.7 Alternative geometry: Moho peeling (restored from v1 Section 13.6.1)
v1 proposed a second way for the keel to leave. Instead of a dense blob dripping from the keel's base (4.3-4.4), the whole mantle part of the keel **peels off along the Moho** like a flap pulled away from the crust (Bird 1978, 1979, "delamination sensu stricto"). The popular picture is wobbly stilts giving way between the crust and the keel. It is a different geometry from the drip, not a variant of it. **Status: kept as an alternative, not adopted and not rejected.** It has not been tested against the v2 checks (Phase B).

**Geometry** (images: `images/ckdt_moho_peeling_diagram.png` and `.svg`; the drip is `images/ckdt_drip_diagram.png`):
| Layer | Thickness | In miles |
|-------|-----------|----------|
| Crust | 35 km | 21.7 mi |
| Weak Moho transition layer (the surface that peels) | 0.1 to a few km; up to about 20 km in unusual non-cratonic settings; v1 used 2.4 km | 0.06 to a few mi; v1: 1.5 mi |
| Keel mantle that peels off (to a 200 km keel base) | 165 km | 102.5 mi |

**What v1 found** (v1 13.6.1; not re-derived):
- **Baseline speed is better.** Published dynamical models give peeling about 10 times faster than dripping (rates, not ages). That leaves a gap of about 3.9 orders of magnitude to the Flood timescale, against 4-7 for dripping.
- **v1's shear-heating lever does not transfer.** Applied to a layer 0.1-20 km thick it gives absurd local temperatures (594 K at 20 km, over 100,000 K at 0.1 km). It only works for 50-100 km shear zones in the drip geometry.
- **Stress amplification does transfer, but is weaker here.** Peeling rate scales as viscosity^(-2/3), so closing 3.9 orders of timescale needs about 5.8 orders of viscosity reduction. The modest 5× stress boost (4 MPa on 1 MPa) gives only 1.6 orders. Only the extreme combination (50 MPa stress drop on 1 MPa, 51×) gives 4.0 orders, just clearing the target.
- **Trigger unresolved**, same as the drip (O4). The literature's own examples (slab rollback, subduction migration, strain migrating in) are external.

**Requirements (v1):** (1) the mantle lithosphere must already be thin enough that hot asthenosphere sits next to the weak Moho layer; (2) the Moho or lower-crust layer must be mechanically weak (hot or hydrated).

**New observations from merging with v2 (my reasoning, not yet checked against data):**
1. **The buoyancy-sign problem applies equally.** Removing the whole 165 km mantle keel with a net-dense excess of +0.8 to +1.2% (Section 14.2) would raise the surface by about 1.3 to 2.0 km (0.8 to 1.2 mi) afterwards. Subsidence would occur only while the flap is peeling. So peeling gives the same sink-then-rebound behaviour as CKDT-II, not a permanent drop.
2. **Requirement (1) conflicts with an intact keel.** A craton with its full 150-250 km keel has no asthenosphere beside the Moho. Peeling can only begin where the keel is already thinned (for example near margins or after a first partial loss). So it cannot be the first failure on an intact craton. It may instead be a second stage after a drip or stretching has thinned the keel.
3. **Weak Moho layer:** no data in the model say the Moho layer under cratons is weak or wet. Cratonic lower crust is generally cold. This is open (O14).

**How it compares with the drip (for Phase B and D):**
| | Drip (4.3-4.4) | Moho peeling (4.7) |
|---|---|---|
| What leaves | Dense lower keel base, 100 km (assumed) | Whole mantle keel, 165 km |
| Where it breaks | Inside the keel | Along the Moho |
| Baseline speed (literature) | slower | about 10× faster |
| Shear-heating lever | works for 50-100 km zones | fails (absurd temperatures) |
| Extra requirement | wet, hot, stressed keel base | pre-thinned keel and weak Moho layer |
| Buoyancy sign | net dense: sink then rebound | same |
| Tested with v2 flow laws | yes | no |

## 5. Companion mechanisms (used only where CKDT cannot cover the budget)

### 5.1 Water from shallow, cool sources (v2 Phase 4)
Present-depth ocean (3.68 km basin): 2.3×10⁸ km³ must start underground. Shallow reservoirs (<5 km) sum to about 1.3×10⁸ km³:
| Reservoir | Size |
|-----------|------|
| Continental groundwater to 2 km (Gleeson et al. 2016) | 2.3×10⁷ km³ |
| Ocean sediment pore water (0.5 km, 40%; my assumption) | 6×10⁷ km³ |
| Serpentinized oceanic lithosphere (5 km, 10%, 13 wt%; my assumption) | 5×10⁷ km³ (to 1.5×10⁸) |

Every 10⁸ km³ released saves about 330 m of subsidence, so 1.3×10⁸ km³ leaves only about 465 m for CKDT and stretching. A water-only flood needs 2.7×10⁸ km³. Deep (transition-zone) water is too hot (H5). The shallow-ocean pre-Flood case needs 78% of all water underground and cannot be met, so **the model uses the present-depth case**. Venting at 100 m/s needs a slot about 1.8 m wide along 99,700 km of ridge.

### 5.2 Handoff to DTAT (note only)
Continent-making is the job of a separate theory, the Differential Terrane Accretion Theory (DTAT; see `Differential Terrane Accretion Theory (DTAT).md` if and when it is written). CKDT stops once subsidence and destabilization are done. DTAT takes over from there. This model gives DTAT no content and no numbers.

Open problems that now belong to DTAT, not CKDT: O1 (new ocean floor), O2 (moving the blocks), O12 (recession and lasting uplift) and O13 (the ten highest peaks). The handoff conditions, such as the state of the crust DTAT inherits, are to be defined when DTAT is written.

## 7. Evidence check: what supports and what cuts against CKDT

### 7.1 Supports
- Keels exist and are large (4.1).
- Stretched, submerged continental crust is real and large (Zealandia, 33% of continental plus transitional crust below sea level).
- Wet power-law creep can make failure fast (4.4).
- Energy stays deep (Section 6).

### 7.2 Cuts against (carried over, not softened)
- **Sign of buoyancy** is unsettled and the original claim fails on it (4.3).
- **No trigger** (4.6).
- **Stalling** is real in published models (4.6).
- **Cross-craton propagation:** the 1,250 km triggering distance is for brittle crustal faults. Transfer to ductile mantle creep is unproven.
- **Water content:** fast failure needs wet keel base, but cratonic keels are generally described as dry and stiff, which is part of why they survive. *(v1 and v2 do not resolve this; flagged here.)*

### 7.3 The xenolith test (v1 13.6)
Cool, conductive xenolith geotherms show no recent catastrophic heating. Fine depth structure (mid-lithospheric discontinuities) appears at Siberian (Liu et al. 2022; 165 xenoliths), Slave (300+ xenoliths) and Kaapvaal cratons and is explained by ancient metasomatism. Kaapvaal's samples reach only 150-200 km versus a keel base of 220-240 km, so the deepest range is a genuine blind spot. Status: **unfavorable where sampled, with a documented gap at the depth that matters.** Caveat: "ancient" in those papers is a deep-time interpretation (P1). The data themselves (temperatures, compositions) are usable; the age reading is not. Phase B must redo this check with that separation.

### 14.2 What Phase A changes
1. **Stretching numbers corrected** by −4.6% (row 20). Conclusions unchanged: β of about 1.5-1.6 explains the transitional crust.
2. **Plate-speed multiples corrected** (row 21): 23,800× for a 4,370-year drift. This makes O2 slightly worse.
3. **Keel sign narrowed.** Sourced chemical depletion (row 14) plus the thermal excess (rows 12-13) gives a net-dense keel of +0.8 to +1.2%, or zero if the isopycnic view holds. Nothing sourced supports a net-light keel. CKDT-II (dense drip) is the only consistent form, with ε of 0 to about 1.2%. If ε is near 0, CKDT-II's subsidence is near 0 too.
4. **The fast wet-failure case is harder than v2 said.** The 66-day result needs about 60 ppm water and 1,650 K (rows 11, 17). The keel base is about 1,563 K, and Kaapvaal's base measures <10 ppm. Scaling the flow law by C_OH^1.2 (same stress and temperature) makes 10 ppm about 9× slower, so about 590 days, still far below millions of years but outside the 150-day window. Siberian samples (up to 323 ppm) are wetter than the wet case. The result depends on which craton and on how much heating precedes failure. This is now O3.
5. **Still unverified, passed to later phases:** slab pull, solidus, dry activation volume, mean keel temperature deficit, sediment and serpentinite reservoirs, the 40 °C cap, cubit, McKenzie against the original paper, the province count.
6. **Deep-time wording to avoid.** Several sources explain their data with deep-time ages (Zealandia thinning, keels "foundering at about 1 km per Gyr"). Only the measured quantities are used; the ages are not.
