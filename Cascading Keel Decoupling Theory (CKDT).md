## 1. Purpose and scope

**Purpose.** Show one physically possible way Noah's Flood could have happened without Baumgardner's Catastrophic Plate Tectonics (CPT) and its "heat problem" (runaway subduction and the rapid making of today's ocean floor, which put 10²⁸-10²⁹ J into the ocean in about a year and boil it).

**Core claim, current state.** The original claim was that continents sit on deep, cold mantle roots (keels), and that the dense lower part of a keel breaks away and sinks, pulling the land down so that it floods without new ocean floor. **That claim is struck** (Section 4.3): any mechanism that removes keel material leaves a keel thinner than the 150-250 km observed under cratons today, which conflicts with premise P9. What remains is the flood geometry (about 0.91 km of subsidence floods a 610 m peak), shallow cool released water (Section 5.1), and the heat constraints. The subsidence mechanism for the keel-bearing continental interiors is **vacant** (O15). The one untested idea is collapse or densification of a layer at the crust base, which removes no keel.

**What CKDT is for, and what it is not for.**
- **It is a flood (submergence) mechanism.** It lowers land relative to sea level. It adds no water and makes no ocean floor.
- **It is not a continent-making or continental-drift mechanism.** CKDT covers only subsidence and destabilization. Making the continents, moving them, and everything after that is handed to DTAT (Section 5.2). Opening the Atlantic and Indian oceans (H9) and moving continents 3,800 km (H10) are therefore outside CKDT (Section 9, items O1 and O2), and the heat limits H9 and H10 still have to be met by DTAT. Any version of this model that claims CKDT also solves them is wrong by v2's results.

## 3. The heat problem and the constraints it sets

### 3.1 The problem
Moving continents and making ocean floor in about a year turns stored thermal and gravitational energy into heat. Heat leaves the ocean slowly (radiation) and rock gives up heat slowly (conduction). Both are rate problems: too fast in, too slow out.

### 3.2 Numbers (v2 Phase 1; sources in v2 `sources/README.md`, S01-S04)
| Quantity | Value |
|----------|-------|
| Ocean heat capacity | 5.5×10²⁴ J/K |
| Energy to warm ocean 15 → 100 °C / to boil it | 4.75×10²⁶ J / 3.5×10²⁷ J |
| **Adopted heat budget B** (ocean limited to 40 °C bulk, my assumption) | **1.4×10²⁶ J** (comfort tier 30 °C: 8.4×10²⁵ J) |
| Fresh hot oceanic lithosphere allowed at 3.9×10¹⁴ J/m² | 3.6×10¹¹ m² (0.10-0.12% of today's ocean floor) |
| Runaway subduction (Baumgardner) | 1×10²⁸ J = 72 B (v2 recomputed the "equivalents" to about 3×10²⁸ J) |
| Making today's ocean floor in the Flood | 1.4×10²⁹ J = about 1,000 B |
| LIPs, gross | 4×10²⁶ J = 2.9 B |
| Conduction time through thickness z (t = z²/κ, κ = 10⁻⁶ m²/s) | 1 m: 12 d; 10 m: 3.2 yr; 100 m: 320 yr; 1 km: 3×10⁴ yr; 10 km: 3×10⁶ yr; 100 km: 3×10⁸ yr |

### 3.3 Design constraints (all later sections must satisfy these)
- **H1 (area).** Fresh hot oceanic lithosphere exposed to seawater ≲ 3.6×10¹¹ m².
- **H2 (rate).** Heat into the ocean during the Flood ≲ 4.4×10¹⁸ W averaged (about 25 times sunlight).
- **H3 (location).** A heat-releasing process must dissipate deeper than about 10 km, or it counts against H1 and H2.
- **H4 (no fresh-seafloor shortcut).** Today's ocean floor cannot have been freshly made from hot mantle during the Flood.
- **H5 (water temperature).** Released water must average ≤ 159 °C (present-depth ocean) or ≤ 47 °C (shallow pre-Flood ocean). That means source depth ≲ 5 km or ≲ 1 km.
- **H6 (water budget).** Water released plus Pangea subsidence must satisfy D = max(0, (h_max + 6.9 m − f·h_P − V_r/A_Earth) / (1 − f)).
- **H7 (keel sign).** Struck in draft 5: no keel-removal mechanism is considered (P9).
- **H8 (subsidence budget).** Released water and the collapse mechanism (or any new mechanism) must together meet the H6 line. Stretching is not a flood source (Section 16).
- **H9 (new floor).** The model must say how the 1.64×10⁸ km² of ocean floor on continent-bearing plates arose within H1-H3.
- **H10 (motion).** The model must name the energy and weak layer that move blocks about 3,800 km, with dissipation staying deep and below melting.
- **H11 (keel preservation, from P9).** After the Flood, every craton must still have a keel within the observed 150-250 km range. Any mechanism must state how much keel it leaves.

## 4. Subsidence mechanisms

v1 and v2 disagree on several points (Section 8). Where they conflict, this section uses the version that survived the harder test, and says so.

### 4.1 Keels exist and are large
- Hasterok et al. (2022) gives 114 craton polygons covering 4.08×10⁷ km² (8% of Earth, 24% of continental-province area). At 150-250 km thick (Pearson et al. 2021) that is 6.1-10.2×10⁹ km³. *(v2 5.5; checked against dataset)*
- Seismic tomography and mantle xenoliths show cold, depleted peridotite roots 150-300+ km deep. *(v1 13.0; literature, not re-verified)*

### 4.2 How much subsidence is needed: about 1 km, not 3-9 km
v1 assumed 3,000-9,000 m of subsidence was needed. **v2's geometry replaces that.** With Pangea covering 41.3% of the Earth (2.105×10⁸ km²) and mean elevation 200 m, covering a 610 m peak by 15 cubits (6.86 m) needs **D ≈ 910 m** of uniform subsidence (839-980 m for 100-300 m mean elevation), with ocean floor fixed. Sea level elsewhere falls 220-360 m. *(v2 3.4; arithmetic checked in v2)*

This lowers CKDT's burden by a factor of 3-10 and is the main reason the model is more credible than v1's.

### 4.3 Keel drip: struck under P9
The keel-drip mechanism was struck in draft 5. A dense lower keel base breaking away and sinking removes keel material. Removing the 0.1-0.9 km of subsidence the model needs takes tens of km of keel (surface change = ε·t, with a net density excess ε of 0.3-1%), and the drip as drawn left about 65 km of a 200 km keel. Observed cratons have 150-250 km today (Section 4.1), so it conflicts with P9. It would only be allowed if the pre-Flood keel were thicker than any observed keel, and nothing in the data supports that.

Findings from the drip work that remain useful for any future keel-related idea:
- A keel that is net dense (chemical depletion 1.1-1.5%, thermal excess about 2.3%) would raise the surface when removed, not lower it (Section 14.2).
- v1's original claim of 1,000-6,000 m of subsidence ignored the thermal excess and is withdrawn.
- The fast-failure analysis (wet olivine power-law creep, shear heating, stress-transfer cascade) belonged only to the drip. It is not carried forward. It remains in v2 `Model.md` Phase 5 and v1 `Model.md` Sections 13.2-13.5.

### 4.4 Struck with the drip
The fast-failure creep and shear-heating analysis (formerly Section 4.4) is removed. See 4.3.

### 4.5 Stretching: a note on geological provinces (not a flood source)
**Note.** Stretching (thinning of the crust and lithosphere by pulling it apart) may help to define and explain the major geological provinces, including Zealandia. It is not part of the flood mechanism. In this model all flood subsidence comes from released water and the collapsed Moho layer (Section 4.8). Phase B step 2 (Section 16) showed stretching floods the margins but cannot help flood the interior: lowering the margins pulls water out of the ocean and lowers sea level, so the interior must drop 60-147 m more (108 m more at its highest ground). Without stretching, the margins need another source (O16).

What the stretching idea explains (from v2 Phase 5; the idea was added in v2, not part of the original theory):
- McKenzie-type initial subsidence (textbook form; v2's code applied the factor (1 − αT_m) twice, overstating results by 4.6%, corrected in Phase A): β = 1.2 → 722 m; β = 1.5 → 1,445 m; β = 2 → 2,167 m (water-loaded; 35 km crust, 125 km lithosphere).
- The dataset's transitional crust (136 provinces, 3.8×10⁷ km², 18% of continental plus transitional) has mean elevation −1,360 m and is 92.6% submerged, a fall of about 1,560 m from a +200 m start, consistent with β of about 1.5-1.6 (corrected values).
- Zealandia (4.9×10⁶ km², about 94% submerged; Mortimer et al. 2017) is the real example of a stretched, permanently submerged continent. Other submerged named fragments (Section 5.2's handoff to DTAT) may fit the same picture.

**Not checked:** how much time the stretching took, and whether any stretched margin sits on a craton keel (which would matter for P9). Stretching is not used in the water budget or the energy table.

### 4.6 Trigger (open for any mechanism)
Whatever replaces the drip still needs something to start it and a way to spread it. The drip-specific analysis (stress-transfer cascade, stalling, cross-craton propagation) is struck. Triggers checked in v1 and found not to work for keel failure: eclogitization (acts on mafic lower crust, needs prior thickening), plume arrival (10-30 My by default). Eclogitization of lower crust is relevant again only for the untested crust-base collapse idea (O15). See Section 9, item O4.

### 4.7 Moho peeling: struck under P9
Moho peeling (the keel's mantle peeling off along the Moho; v1 Section 13.6.1) was restored in draft 3 and struck in draft 4. It removes the whole 165 km keel mantle, so it leaves no keel and conflicts with P9. v1's analysis remains in `Noah's Pangea v1/Model.md` Section 13.6.1 for reference. The drip and peeling images were deleted in draft 5 (recoverable from git history). `images/` now holds the numbered diagrams for the idea in 4.8 (01-03).

### 4.8 Candidate: collapse of a thick layer at the crust base (untested)
The one idea left that satisfies P9. A layer between the crust and the keel was thicker before the Flood and collapses to its present thickness in a cascade. The crust drops onto the keel, which is not touched. Images (numbered for sequential viewing, each as `.png` and `.svg`): `images/01_collapse_overview` (before, during, after), `images/02_craton_vs_margin` (where it can act and where it cannot, and the unresolved fluid source), `images/03_cascade_loop` (the five-step cascade with evidence levels), `images/04_craton_moho_pressure` (craton Moho pressure and lower-crust thickness against the eclogite field, from Section 19).

**Arithmetic (mine, not checked against data).** Subsidence equals the thickness the layer loses, because mass is conserved and the keel does not move. Taking today's layer as 2.4 km (v1's estimate; published Moho transition zones run 0.1 to a few km):
| Case | Subsidence needed | Layer before | Layer after | Collapse |
|------|-------------------|--------------|-------------|----------|
| No released water | 0.91 km (0.57 mi) | 3.31 km (2.06 mi) | 2.4 km (1.5 mi) | 27% |
| With 1.3×10⁸ km³ shallow water released (5.1) | 0.48 km (0.30 mi) | 2.88 km (1.79 mi) | 2.4 km (1.5 mi) | 17% |

**Why it is not yet a mechanism:**
1. **No pore space to crush.** Rock at about 35 km depth (about 1 GPa) has almost no void space. A 16-27% collapse needs a density-raising change in the rock itself. The usual example is gabbro turning to eclogite, which is roughly 10-15% denser (from memory, unverified). A layer of 3-9 km fully converted would give 0.46-0.91 km. That is thicker than the measured layer today.
2. **Trigger, spread and speed are unknown** (O4). Fluids can start such reactions and cold, dry lower crust can stay unreacted for a long time (from memory, not checked), but cratonic lower crust is cold and the reaction is slow in dry rock.
3. **It has to happen under all the land**, not only cratons. The model has no data on lower crust everywhere.
4. **What it would leave today:** a dense layer that seismics might read as mantle. Reported high-velocity lower-crust layers at the base of many cratons are a possible match, not checked.

**Heat (rough):** crust sinking about 0.9 km releases about 1.5×10²⁶ J over all land, about 1.1 times the heat budget B, at about 35 km depth (conduction time about 40 million years, so it passes H3).

**Status (updated after Phase B step 1, Section 15):** not ruled out, not shown. The 27% collapse and 3.3 km layer in the table and image above **exceed what a density-raising phase change gives** (9-17% thickness loss). If the collapse is a phase change, the layer must have been about 5.5-10 km thick (2.9-5.3 km with released water) and be about 5-9 km thick today. The overview image `images/01_collapse_overview.png` was redrawn with the corrected middle case (7.0 km before, 6.1 km after, 13% collapse, 15% density increase).

**Update after Phase B steps 6-9 (Sections 20-24):**
- **Gabbro version (candidate A):** the eclogite boundary for basic rock is about 1.9 GPa at high temperature (Chapman et al. 2017) and needs more than 600-800 °C plus fluid in cool lithosphere (Buntin et al. 2021). The craton Moho is at a median 1.07 GPa and about 500 °C (Section 24, inputs assumed). It is the weakest candidate (O18).
- **Serpentinite version (candidate B, user's suggestion):** a fragile, serpentinized layer. Dehydration (antigorite = forsterite + talc + water, the reaction below 2 GPa) shrinks the solid 23-25%, so 2-4 km of layer would be enough; trapped hydrogen adds at most about 2%. Subsidence needs the released water to escape through cracks. The layer is not shown to exist under cratons, and the craton Moho is 100-150 °C below the antigorite limit (Sections 21 and 24). Status: unresolved, a candidate, not supported.
- **Both candidates need heat at the craton Moho** (O1, O2, O20).

Present-depth ocean (3.68 km basin): 2.3×10⁸ km³ must start underground. Shallow reservoirs (<5 km) sum to about 1.3×10⁸ km³:
| Reservoir | Size |
|-----------|------|
| Continental groundwater to 2 km (Gleeson et al. 2016) | 2.3×10⁷ km³ |
| Ocean sediment pore water (0.5 km, 40%; my assumption) | 6×10⁷ km³ |
| Serpentinized oceanic lithosphere (5 km, 10%, 13 wt%; my assumption) | 5×10⁷ km³ (to 1.5×10⁸) |

Every 10⁸ km³ released saves about 330 m of subsidence, so 1.3×10⁸ km³ leaves only about 475 m for the collapse mechanism. A water-only flood needs 2.7×10⁸ km³. Deep (transition-zone) water is too hot (H5). The shallow-ocean pre-Flood case needs 78% of all water underground and cannot be met, so **the model uses the present-depth case**. Venting at 100 m/s needs a slot about 1.8 m wide along 99,700 km of ridge.

## 7. Evidence check: what supports and what cuts against CKDT

### 7.1 Supports
- Keels exist and are large (4.1), and cool xenolith geotherms say they are intact today. This is consistent with P9.
- Stretched, submerged continental crust is real and large (Zealandia, 33% of continental plus transitional crust below sea level). This supports the province note in 4.5, not the flood mechanism.
- Energy released deep stays deep (Section 15.2).

### 7.2 Cuts against
- **No keel-based subsidence mechanism survives P9.** The subsidence for keel-bearing interiors is vacant (O15).
- **No trigger** for any mechanism (4.6).

### 7.3 The xenolith test (v1 13.6)
Cool, conductive xenolith geotherms show no recent catastrophic heating. Fine depth structure (mid-lithospheric discontinuities) appears at Siberian (Liu et al. 2022; 165 xenoliths), Slave (300+ xenoliths) and Kaapvaal cratons and is explained by ancient metasomatism. Kaapvaal's samples reach only 150-200 km versus a keel base of 220-240 km, so the deepest range is a genuine blind spot. Status: **unfavorable where sampled, with a documented gap at the depth that matters.** Caveat: "ancient" in those papers is a deep-time interpretation (P1). The data themselves (temperatures, compositions) are usable; the age reading is not. Phase B must redo this check with that separation. Under P9 this evidence now supports the model, because it says keels are intact today.

### 15.2 Arithmetic (mine)
Subsidence equals the thickness the layer loses (mass is conserved and the keel does not move). A phase change that raises density by fraction d shrinks the layer by d/(1+d).
| Density increase | Thickness loss | Layer needed for 0.91 km | Layer needed for 0.48 km (with shallow water) |
|------------------|----------------|--------------------------|-----------------------------------------------|
| 10% | 9.1% | 10.0 km (6.2 mi) | 5.3 km (3.3 mi) |
| 15% | 13.0% | 7.0 km (4.3 mi) | 3.7 km (2.3 mi) |
| 20% | 16.7% | 5.5 km (3.4 mi) | 2.9 km (1.8 mi) |

- **Correction to Section 4.8 and the image.** A 27% collapse of a 3.3 km layer is more than a phase change can give. The layer must have been about 3-10 km thick before and about 2.5-9 km thick after. A thickness after collapse of 5-9 km is in the range of the 6-8 km high-velocity bodies reported in the Fennoscandian Shield, but it is thicker than the 0.1-few km Moho transition zone, so "the layer we see today" would be the eclogitized layer, not the thin Moho transition.
- **Speed gap:** 20 thousand to 4 million years against 150 days is 4.7 to 7.0 orders of magnitude too slow. It is the same kind of gap the keel drip had.
- **Heat:** sinking the crust 0.91 km releases about 1.5×10²⁶ J over all continental crust (1.1 B), 3.6×10²⁵ J over cratons alone (0.25 B), at about 35 km depth, where conduction takes about 40 million years. It passes H3.
- **Where the layer is eclogite-stable:** 35-40 km is 1.0-1.2 GPa. Only a cold lower crust (cratons) reaches the eclogite field there. So the idea would work mainly under cratons, which is where stretching does not act and where P9 forbids removing keel. Warm lower crust (stretched or young regions) would stay granulite. This is my inference and is unchecked.

### 15.3 Verdict
**Not ruled out, not shown.** It satisfies P9, it targets the keel-bearing interiors that stretching cannot reach, it passes H3, and a similar structure (a partly eclogitized layer 6-8 km thick) has been reported in one craton. It is weakest on three points:
1. **Speed:** 4.7-7 orders too slow on indirect numbers. Fluid supply is the controlling factor, and the model's released shallow water is a possible but unexamined source. Revised in Section 17: the best-case literature (weeks) fits the window.
2. **Thickness:** it needs a 3-10 km mafic layer at the crust base before the Flood, and whether the lower crust is mafic at all is disputed.
3. **Coverage:** it probably works only where the lower crust is cold.

## 17. Phase B step 3: can the collapse reaction be fast enough? (2026-11-24)

**Question.** Section 15 found the gabbro-to-eclogite reaction 4.7-7 orders too slow on indirect numbers. Is there a physical route that closes the gap? **Method.** Web search of abstracts and summaries (not full papers) plus arithmetic. Codes as in Section 14.

### 17.1 Findings
| # | Finding | Code |
|---|---------|------|
| 1 | Ahrens & Schubert (1975): dry solid-state diffusion cannot finish the transformation in "geologically meaningful" time below about 600-800 °C. They propose a water-film mechanism in which the reaction proceeds rapidly if water pressure is at least about 0.5-1 kbar, with ion solubility the rate-limiting step. They say almost no direct rate data existed beyond rough times at two temperatures (Ito & Kennedy 1971). | C |
| 2 | Natural examples (Bergen Arcs): the transformation stays overstepped until fluid arrives. One study says the reaction was limited by dissolution-precipitation kinetics, not by pressure-temperature. A separate abstract on partially eclogitized slab gabbros (not the Bergen Arcs) gives "a few weeks" (conference abstract) or days to "a few months" (full paper) for fluid flow through a shear zone (see 17.7 and 17.8 for what that does and does not cover). | C |
| 3 | Lower-crustal earthquakes in dry, strong granulite are described as the first step: faulting and melting produce fine fragments and fluid pathways, and metamorphism and shear zones follow. | C |
| 4 | A proposed feedback: the volume decrease of the reaction creates porosity that keeps the fluid pathways open until the whole rock is converted. | C |
| 5 | Laboratory work: deformation speeds the reaction compared with static runs (preliminary); fluid-present runs at 700 °C and 2 GPa reproduce natural textures (durations not seen); an albite-to-jadeite reaction ran quickly at 400-500 °C both with and without water (preliminary). | C |
| 6 | Heat from the reaction is mentioned as a possible accelerator ("thermal runaway" is cited as one idea, mechanisms unclear). Not quantified. | U |
| 7 | Indirect duration estimates remain 20 thousand years to 1-4 million years (Section 15.1), some from age-based methods that P1 does not accept. | C |

### 17.2 Arithmetic (mine)
- 150 days = 1.3×10⁷ s. "Weeks" (2-4 weeks) is 1.2-2.4×10⁶ s, so about 5-10 times faster than the window. The indirect estimates (20 thousand years to 4 million years) are 4.7-7.0 orders slower.
- So the speed gap ranges from **none** (best-case, fluid-saturated shear zones, partial conversion) to **7 orders** (indirect estimates). The reaction cannot be called too slow, but it has not been shown fast over a whole craton.

### 17.3 What the findings add to the model
1. **A physical cascade.** Findings 3 and 4 give a loop: a lower-crustal earthquake opens fluid pathways → eclogitization begins → the layer densifies and shrinks (crust drops, and porosity opens) → the stress change loads neighbouring crust → the next earthquake. This is my synthesis from the cited findings, not a result in any one source. It would give the theory's "cascade" a basis, and it addresses the earlier worry (Section 15) that earthquake triggering describes brittle rock. Cold, dry lower crust under cratons is brittle (observed in the Bergen Arcs). It would also supply a candidate trigger for O4.
2. **Fluid supply is now the controlling unknown (O17).** The reaction needs water in contact with dry granulite at 35-42 km. The shallow water in Section 5.1 is released near the surface, so a source or path to the crust base is not identified. The heat of any deep fluid stays deep (H3), so hot water at that depth is not excluded by the model's own limits. Whether deep mantle water could reach the crust base (Genesis 7:11, "fountains of the great deep") is not examined here. The earlier model (v2) rejected deep water only because it would reach the ocean too hot.
3. **Coverage is still limited.** The loop works where the lower crust is cold, dry and brittle, so it does not cover the warm margins (O16).

### 17.4 Verdict
**The speed objection weakens but is not closed.** Best-case literature (about 7-14 days of fluid flow through one shear zone, at 820 °C and 90-110 km, in one study; see 17.8 for its limits) fits the 150-day window. Typical estimates do not. The reaction can be fast where fluid and fracturing are present. Whether that holds across a whole craton depends on fluid supply, which is now the weakest point (O17).

### 17.7 Addendum: the primary source behind the "weeks" figure (2026-11-24)
**Source.** Smit, M. A. and Pogge von Strandmann, P., "Rapid reaction-induced breaching of the slab lower crust sparks deep fluid release beneath arcs", GSA Annual Meeting 2020, abstract 109-7 (doi 10.1130/abs/2020AM-357180). The abstract cites a full paper, Smit & Pogge von Strandmann (2020), *Earth and Planetary Science Letters* 534, 116046, which I have not read.

**Exact claim.** "Lithium chronometry indicates that it took only a few weeks for the shear zone network to develop and for the externally derived fluids to traverse this network and drive eclogitization."

**What it covers, and what it does not**
- It covers the time for a shear-zone network to form and for fluids to cross it and drive the reaction, in gabbros that were **partially** transformed to hydrous ultrahigh-pressure eclogite along shear zones. The degree of conversion is not quantified.
- It does not give the time to convert a whole 3-10 km layer by 9-17%.
- The rocks are from a subducting slab, with re-equilibration at about 90 km depth. The CKDT layer is at 35-42 km under a cold craton. The locality is not named in the abstract.
- The abstract frames its larger conclusion as a hypothesis ("It is possible that...", "Our data suggest that...").
- The method is named only as "Li chemical and isotope chronometry". The abstract gives no model. Whether it is diffusion-based (a rate, acceptable under P1) or not is not stated; lithium chronometry is usually diffusion-based, but that is my inference.

**Conflicting evidence.** A 2025 study of Holsnøy (Bergen Arcs) garnets reports that diffusion-based estimates for that rock span hundreds of years to millions of years and that the metamorphism lasted longer than 10⁶ years with short, pulsed eclogite episodes. So "a few weeks" describes one stage in particular rocks, not the whole transformation.

**A useful analog for O17.** The same abstract argues that fluids from the slab mantle accumulate beneath the Moho until the lower crust is breached by dynamic fluid vents, after which the delayed transformation starts. That is a fluid-supply-and-breach mechanism at the crust base. Its fluid comes from slab dehydration, which a craton interior does not have, so CKDT would still need its own source.

**Effect on the verdict.** The speed gap is narrower than "4.7 to 7 orders" only for the network-and-fluid-traversal stage. The time to complete the conversion over a craton is still unmeasured. The "weeks" figure is a supporting data point, not a measurement of the collapse.

### 17.8 Addendum 2: the full paper (2026-11-24)
**Source read.** Smit & Pogge von Strandmann, "Deep fluid release in warm subduction zones from a breached slab seal" (accepted manuscript of *EPSL* 534, 116046, 2020; the open-access PDF's metadata carries a different title but its text matches). I read the abstract, Section 5.2 (duration) and the methods; the figures and the full discussion I did not study.

**What the study is.** Eclogitized gabbros from the Western Gneiss Complex, Norway (the Seiholmen occurrence), exhumed from a Caledonian ultrahigh-pressure setting. Peak conditions about 820 °C and 3.0-3.6 GPa (90-110 km depth). These are subducted slab rocks, not Bergen Arcs rocks and not craton lower crust.

**The timescale, as the paper gives it**
| Statement | Where |
|-----------|-------|
| Duration of diffusive Li exchange (the time dynamic fluid-filled porosity existed across the shear zone): 7.1 (+4.3/-3.9) days from the ⁷Li profile and 13.6 (+6.0/-5.4) days from the Li concentration profile | Section 5.2 |
| "Shearing, and by extension the associated reactions, occurred on very short timescales of days, perhaps months" | Section 5.2 |
| "Breached and permeated within a few months" | Abstract |
| The GSA conference abstract's "a few weeks" is a shorter wording of the same result; the paper itself says days to months | 17.7 vs the paper |

**Method (P1 check).** Fick's-law diffusion modelling of a lithium profile: diffusion length scale 0.11-0.13 m, Li diffusivity in the fluid 1.98×10⁻⁸ m²/s (extrapolated from lab data to 820 °C and 3.0-3.6 GPa), rock-fluid partition coefficient 1.19 (from experiments), porosity 3×10⁻³. It is a rate calculation from laboratory diffusion data, not a radiometric age, so it is acceptable under P1. The authors say their estimate may be a maximum (shear heating and later solid-state diffusion would overestimate it), may miss delays in Li supply, and that true uncertainty may be larger than quoted.

**What it supports, and what it does not**
- It supports a rapid breach: dry, rigid gabbro stayed metastable, then a permeable shear-zone network developed and sustained fluid flow for days to months. That matches the idea of a metastable layer followed by a rapid, pulsed transformation.
- It measures how long fluid flowed through a **decimetre-scale** shear zone, not the time to convert a layer kilometres across. The authors note the reaction front outran the lithium diffusion, so the reaction itself was at least that fast in that rock.
- It was at **820 °C**. A cold craton Moho is about 500-600 °C (Section 15.1), 220-320 °C cooler. Reactions slow strongly with falling temperature, so these rates do not transfer directly. The slowing has not been estimated.
- The fluid came from the deserpentinizing slab mantle, trapped at very high pore pressure beneath the slab Moho until the lower crust failed. A craton interior has no such reservoir under its Moho (a cold, depleted keel lies there), so the fluid question (O17) is unchanged.

**Effect on the verdict.** The speed objection is weaker still for the breach stage (days to months at 820 °C) but remains open at craton temperature and over a whole layer. The "weeks" wording in earlier sections should be read as "days to a few months".

**Question.** The best-case timescale (Section 17.8) was measured at about 820 °C. A cold craton Moho is 500-600 °C. **Reading.** With the 7-14 day base the window is met at 550-600 °C for E = 60 and marginally at 600 °C for E = 90. With the "months" base it is met only for very low activation energy. Across all cases the scaled time runs from about 12 days to about 15 years.

### 18.3 What this does not settle, and a new concern
1. **The base time was not a reaction time.** The paper says the reaction front outran the lithium, and that lithium diffusion in the pore fluid set the 7-14 days. So at 820 °C the reaction was faster than that, by an unknown amount. If a temperature-sensitive reaction step was the limit at craton temperature, the scaled times are upper bounds. If fluid supply or fracture-network spacing stays the limit, temperature matters little. Either way the table brackets the possibilities and does not give a measured answer.
2. **The activation energies are borrowed** from feldspar dissolution at much lower temperature. The eclogite reaction may differ by a large factor.
3. **Scale** is still about 0.1 m, not kilometres (Section 17.8).
4. **New concern: the driving force at 35-42 km.** That depth is 1.0-1.25 GPa. Section 15.1 reports cratonic lower crust at 500-600 °C expected to be in the eclogite field at about 1.3-1.6 GPa, and eclogite facies quoted at 1.8-2.2 GPa. At 1.0-1.25 GPa the rock may be near or below the eclogite boundary. If it stops at garnet granulite (3.2-3.3 g/cm³ against a 2.9-3.0 g/cm³ start, about 10% denser), the collapse is at the low end of 9-17%, so the layer must be about 10 km thick for 0.91 km of drop (5.3 km with all shallow water). A deeper layer, or a smaller reaction, is open. This may matter more than the speed.

### 18.4 Verdict
**Speed at craton temperature is not shown to fail, and not shown to work.** The scaled estimates (12 days to 15 years for a decimetre-scale zone) span the 150-day window and are built on borrowed activation energies. The decisive unknowns are now the driving force at 35-42 km (O18) and the fluid supply (O17). Together with the unshown mafic layer (Section 15), these are the three conditions most likely to decide whether the idea survives.

## 19. Phase B step 5: does the craton Moho lie in the eclogite field? (2026-11-24)

**Question.** Section 18 raised O18: at 35-42 km (1.0-1.25 GPa) a cold craton lower crust may be below the pressure where eclogite is stable. How deep and how high-pressure are real craton Mohos, and how thick is the lower crust? **Method.** I computed it from data already in the project: CRUST1.0 (crustal boundaries and layer densities on a 1° grid; `Noah's Pangea v1/data/reference/crust1/`) and the 114 craton polygons of Hasterok et al. ### 19.2 What this means
1. **The layer thickness requirement is met.** The model needs a 2.9-10 km layer (Sections 15.2 and 16.3). CRUST1.0 gives a lower crust 8-15 km thick (median 12.5 km) over essentially all craton area, with a density of about 2.9-3.0 g/cm³, in the gabbro range. That helps the "layer exists" requirement. Caveats: CRUST1.0 is a seismic model with assumed velocity-density relations, and whether the lower crust is mafic is disputed (Section 15.1). The "layer" is therefore the lower part of the lower crust, not a separate thin Moho layer.
2. **The pressure is mostly too low.** The median Moho pressure is 1.07 GPa, and only 3% of craton area reaches 1.3 GPa. Section 15.1 cites cratonic lower crust at 500-600 °C expected in the eclogite field at about 1.3-1.6 GPa, and eclogite facies quoted at 1.8-2.2 GPa. By that boundary, about 97% of craton area has its Moho below the eclogite field, and the rest of the lower crust is shallower still (its top is about 0.7 GPa for a 12.5 km layer).
3. **The boundary is uncertain.** One source in Section 15.1 describes conditions at about 1 GPa (35-40 km) as inside the eclogite field on a cratonic geotherm for a particular rock. Composition, temperature and kinetics all move the boundary. The test shows the Moho is mostly near the low-pressure edge, not that eclogite cannot form.
4. **If eclogite is not reached,** the product is garnet granulite (3.2-3.3 g/cm³, about 10% denser than a 2.9-3.0 starting rock). That is the low end of 9-17%, so the layer must be about 10 km for 0.91 km of drop, or 5.3 km with all shallow water (Section 15.2). The 12.5 km median lower crust fits those thicknesses, but only if essentially all of it converts, which is less likely at these pressures.
5. **Fluids cannot drive a reaction that is not thermodynamically favoured.** The reaction needs a pressure or temperature overstep. Where the rock sits below the eclogite boundary there is none to drive.

### 19.3 Verdict
**This check hurts, and O18 is now the main weakness.** The layer is thick enough (a point in favour), but about 97% of craton area has its Moho below the pressure at which one cited source expects eclogite at craton temperature. Unless the boundary is lower than that source says for these rocks, the reaction stops at garnet granulite or does not start. The model's best outcome is the low end of the density range: a collapse of about 9-10% rather than 13-17%, needing most of the lower crust to convert.

## 21. Phase B step 7: serpentinite as the collapsing layer (2026-11-24, rewritten after discussion)

**Question.** Could a fragile, serpentinized layer at the crust base be the layer that collapses (Section 4.8), in place of gabbro (Sections 15-20)? **Premise as stated by the user:** (1) catastrophic cracks let the water out, and (2) hydrogen and methane trapped in the serpentinized rock make it compressible. The layer's collapse is the subsidence; water release is a consequence, not a separate driver.

**Constraint on any version.** The keel does not move and the layer's mass is conserved, so the crust can only drop if the layer loses volume. A weak layer that flows sideways only moves material.

### 21.1 The ways a fragile layer can lose volume
| Route | Volume loss | Status |
|-------|-------------|--------|
| A. Dehydration (antigorite breaks down, water leaves) | 23-25% of the solid; if the water stays in the rock the total volume is 0 to 5% larger, not smaller (21.2) | Large enough; needs the water to escape and heat or a pressure change |
| B. Release of trapped hydrogen and methane (21.3) | at most about 2% | Too small alone |
| C. Crushing of pore space | very small at 1.0-1.25 GPa (Section 4.8, item 1) | Not a route |
| D. Sideways ductile flow | none in net | Moves rock, no net subsidence |

Routes A and B are linked: cracks that vent water also vent gas.

### 21.2 Route A: dehydration (mine; inputs assumed)
`scripts/serpentinite_volume.py`. **Reaction used (corrected 2026-11-24):** below 2 GPa antigorite breaks down as antigorite = 18 forsterite + 4 talc + 27 H₂O (Perrillat et al. 2005, introduction, pasted by the user; paper behind a paywall). With antigorite Mg₄₈Si₃₄O₈₅(OH)₆₂ (4,535.9 g/mol), the script checks that this balances. It releases 27 of the 31 water molecules, **10.7 wt% water**, not the 12-13 wt% I first used (that is the total structural water, 12.3 wt%, released only by the high-pressure reaction to forsterite + enstatite). Inputs from memory, unverified: antigorite 2.55-2.60 g/cm³, forsterite 3.22, talc 2.75-2.80, free water 1.0-1.1 g/cm³ at about 1 GPa and 600 °C (assumed).
| Quantity | Result |
|----------|--------|
| Solid volume lost on breakdown | **23-25%** |
| Total volume change if the water stays in the rock | **0 to 5% larger** (an expansion, not a loss) |
| Serpentinite needed for 0.91 km of subsidence | 3.6-3.9 km |
| Serpentinite needed for 0.48 km (with shallow water, 5.1) | 1.9-2.1 km |

**Corrections to the first version of this table.** (1) Solid loss was 28-31% because I used the wrong reaction and too much water; with the reaction that applies near the craton Moho it is 23-25%, so the layer needed is 2-4 km, not 1.5-3.6 km. (2) I wrote the "net volume change if the water stays" as "0 to -5%", which read as a shrinkage. The calculation gives the opposite: solid plus free water takes 0 to 5% **more** volume than the serpentinite did. That fits the general finding that dehydration can raise pore pressure and cause embrittlement. It also means that without an exit the layer would swell, not shrink.

This is about 1.5-2.7 times the loss for gabbro to eclogite (9-17%). An unchecked general summary says serpentinization swells rock by up to 40%; the reverse is 1 - 1/1.4 = 29%, a maximum that is higher than the 23-25% stoichiometric figure. **Subsidence happens only if the water leaves the layer.** If it stays, the rock and water together are slightly larger than before, and the water pressure props the rock open. The cracks (premise 1) supply that exit.

**Perrillat et al. (2005, EPSL 236:899-913), from the abstract, introduction and three snippets the user pasted (the paper is paywalled and was not read):** antigorite dehydration was followed in situ by X-ray diffraction at 1.1-5.5 GPa. The high-temperature stability limit is **50-100 °C lower under water-unsaturated (drained) conditions**; breakdown goes through an intermediate forsterite + talc-like stage over about 130 ± 20 °C; overall rates are 10 to 100 times faster than in water-saturated experiments, and the water activity is "a strong driving force for dehydration". The authors estimate a fluid discharge rate of 10⁻⁶ to 10⁻⁸ s⁻¹, faster than viscous relaxation of serpentinite, so brittle failure could result. Their sample was a natural Cuban serpentinite with 3.45 wt% Al₂O₃ and 4.15 wt% FeO(total), and at 3.6 GPa the limit was 580 °C. The pasted text does not contain the 570 °C at 1.1 GPa that Gemba and Nakamura attribute to this paper, so that figure remains second-hand. **Consequences:** (1) the limit near the craton Moho may be lower still if cracks drain the layer, which would favour a cold craton even more, though I cannot combine the 50-100 °C with the 500-550 °C figure without the paper; (2) fast breakdown once started, with embrittlement by the released fluid, supports a cascading trigger (O4), the first mechanism the model has for one failure loading the next.

**Check of a pasted literature summary (2026-11-24).** A search summary gave the pure-system (Mg-Si-H₂O) breakdown to talc + forsterite as about 570-600 °C at 1.0-1.5 GPa (Wunder and Schreyer 1997) and natural, aluminium-bearing antigorite as about 620 °C at 1.0 GPa (Ulmer and Trommsdorff 1995). **Not verified:** I could not read either paper, the numbers were not in any text I read, and the links in the summary did not match the claims (one pointed to a paper on nickel sulfide alteration). What I did verify, from the open Journal of Petrology paper of Eberhard et al. (2023, doi 10.1093/petrology/egad069, read in full): iron (Fe²⁺) lowers antigorite stability (Merkulova et al. 2016), aluminium raises it (Bromiley and Pawley 2003; Padrón-Navarta et al. 2013), the effect of Fe³⁺ has not been examined systematically, and the limits are shown only as curves in figures, with no number near 1 GPa in the text. A 2020s review reached through a search says the thermal stability is about 650 °C at 2-3 GPa. **Working bracket for the limit near 1.0-1.25 GPa:** about 570-620 °C in short, water-saturated experiments, consistent across the Tohoku one-week runs (600-650 °C for natural samples), the 570 °C quoted second-hand for Perrillat (1.1 GPa) and the unverified 570-620 °C above; **500-550 °C is the low outlier** (long runs, iron-bearing, one abstract). Iron lowers the limit and aluminium raises it, so a natural layer's value depends on its composition.

Stability (search snippets only): antigorite breaks down to forsterite + enstatite (or talc) + water, with an upper limit near 680 °C at 1.9 GPa; deformed samples began to dehydrate at 1.5 GPa and 620-670 °C; water-poor conditions lower the limit by 50-100 °C; aluminium and chromium raise it by 60-70 °C. **The limit at 1.2 GPa (found, 2026-11-24):** a 2006 poster abstract (Gemba and Nakamura, JpGU I142-P005, read in full; the full paper was not found) reports piston-cylinder runs of Fe-bearing antigorite (Mg# 89.5-97, synthetic gels and natural powders, water added) at 500-700 °C and 1.2 GPa. In long runs (27,000-40,000 min) antigorite was stable at 500 °C and gone by 550 °C, so **the limit at 1.2 GPa is 500-550 °C**; the talc breakdown (to enstatite + forsterite) falls between 550 and 600 °C; no Mg# dependence was seen. In one-week runs, the usual duration in earlier work, gels did not react at 600 °C and only a little forsterite + talc formed at 625 °C, so the limit appeared about 75 °C higher. The lowest earlier value for talc + forsterite near 1 GPa is 570 °C at 1.1 GPa (Perrillat et al. 2005, quoted in the abstract, not read). The authors say the Fe-bearing limit is 20-70 °C lower than earlier magnesium-silicate-water experiments, from either iron or run length, and that dehydration then occurs over a small temperature range.
- **Consequence:** the limit near the craton Moho pressure (1.0-1.25 GPa) is about 500-550 °C (long runs) or 570-625 °C (short runs), 100-150 °C lower than the 620-680 °C I carried over from 1.5-1.9 GPa. The Moho temperatures in Section 24.6 (below 400 °C Siberia, 500-600 °C Tanzania) then put a Tanzanian-type Moho at or near the limit and a Siberian-type Moho 100-150 °C or more below it. A serpentinite layer at a Tanzanian-type Moho would be at the point of breaking down, so it could not have persisted there in equilibrium; at a Siberian-type Moho it could persist, and a rise of 100-200 °C would trigger it.
- **Limits:** one abstract with no paper, no pressure series (the limit at 1.0 and 1.1 GPa is not given), a limit from run duration that may reflect kinetics, and iron-bearing starting materials. I did not read the earlier experiments it compares against.

### 21.3 Route B: hydrogen and methane (premise 2)
`scripts/gas_volume.py`. **Correction.** In discussion I first said a few tenths of a percent of the rock's mass could be hydrogen, giving about 5% of the volume. The sources show that was too high: hydrogen from serpentinization is limited by the iron in the rock.
- **Ceiling:** about 0.47 mol H₂ per kg of peridotite (10 wt% FeO, all of it oxidized to magnetite), which is about 0.09 wt%. Real yields are lower because the Fe(III)/total iron ratio in serpentinites averages about 0.45. Modelled and measured values are about 0.1-0.35 mol/kg (a Frontiers 2021 study gives 120 to over 300 mmol/kg below 200 °C for some rocks, and 200-350 mmol/kg at slow-spreading ridges).
- **Molar volume at about 1 GPa and 500-600 °C:** not found in any source. I assumed 10-20 cm³/mol (from memory). An ideal gas would give 5 cm³/mol, but real hydrogen is much less compressible at this pressure.
- **Result:** hydrogen fills about **0.2-2.2%** of the serpentinite's volume (2.6 g/cm³ rock, 1.13 kg per kg of peridotite). Gas alone would need a layer of 22-42 km (best case) or 200-400 km (low case) to give 0.48-0.91 km of drop. Methane needs a carbon source and uses four hydrogen molecules per molecule formed, so it adds no more volume than the hydrogen it consumes (my reasoning, not from a source).
- **So the gas is real but small.** It could add one to two percent to route A and could help trigger it (a pressure drop when gas vents), but it cannot carry the collapse.
- **Other findings:** at high hydrogen concentration, hydrogen and methane separate into two fluids (immiscibility) under upper-mantle conditions (2026 EGU abstract), and no hydrogen-methane equation of state validated near 1 GPa and 600 K was found. Pores holding gas at 1 GPa must also stay open against creep, which I have not checked.

### 21.4 Does the layer exist? (against the idea)
- **No evidence found under cratons.** Serpentinized mantle is reported at subduction forearcs (Cascadia inverted Moho), rifted margins (Galicia, Porcupine) and in the Alps, where a serpentinite channel may be only a few km thick.
- **Origin points to the sea floor.** A user-supplied search summary (unsourced) says serpentinization forms at about 100-500 °C where seawater or hydrothermal fluid enters mantle rock through cracks in the ocean floor. Craton mantle below the Moho is cold and dry and has no sea floor above it. The serpentinite would also have to be buried to the crust base with its gas still in it.
- **Real analog at the right depth, but at forearcs (Blakely, Brocher and Wells 2005, Geology 33:445-448; read in full).** Hydrated mantle at about 40 km depth just below the continental Moho is documented in the Cascadia forearc: an "inverted" Moho (higher-velocity crust over lower-velocity mantle) interpreted as 50-60% serpentinized (Bostock et al. 2002), density falling from above 3,000 to about 2,500 kg/m³, and magnetic susceptibility rising by several orders of magnitude. The water comes from the subducting plate, whose metabasalt turns to eclogite at 40-50 km. The paper's own conclusion is that magnetic mantle "may be common in forearc settings"; it does not examine old crust or cratons. Magnetite loses its magnetization above about 580 °C, so a hot Moho would show no magnetic signal even if serpentinite were present. This supports the Section 21.4 reading: the process exists at the right depth, but where a slab supplies the water, not in craton interiors.
- One craton study (2020, mid-lithosphere low-velocity zones at 60-150 km) lists hydrous phases as a candidate, not serpentine and not at the Moho. CRUST1.0 lower crust looks gabbroic (Section 19), so the seismic data do not call for serpentinite.

### 21.5 What the layer's weakness does and does not do
A weak, low-friction layer fails easily under stress, so one failure can load the next. That helps the cascade and the trigger (O4). It does not supply volume loss, which only routes A and B do.

### 21.6 Verdict
**Better on volume than gabbro, worse on existence. Status: unresolved, a candidate, not supported.** Route A is large enough (2-4 km of layer) and releases water, but it needs the layer to exist, the water to escape, and likely added heat. The gas (route B) is at most about 2% and cannot carry the collapse. The idea fits margins and forearcs better than craton interiors, which bears on the O16 coverage gap.

### 21.7 Follow-up
- **Antigorite limit near 1.2 GPa: found (Section 21.2).** About 500-550 °C in long runs and 570-625 °C in one-week runs (Gemba and Nakamura 2006 abstract, Fe-bearing antigorite, water added). Still missing: the limit at 1.0 and 1.1 GPa, and the refereed paper. ## 22. Phase B step 8: crustal cracks as province boundaries and vent routes (2026-11-24, FIRST PASS)

### 22.2 What this shows and does not show
1. **Reach.** Mapped province boundaries are dense. Almost all land lies within 250-400 km of one, so a water route along them would not need very long lateral paths. Craton and shield interiors are the farthest (median 111 km, 95% within 396 km), which matches the expectation that they are the hardest places to vent, but the gap is modest.
2. **Weak test.** Because the boundaries are so dense, nearly any set of cracks would pass this check. It does not show that cracks follow these lines, only that using them would not be geometrically impossible.
3. **Not the same as sutures.** Province boundaries include rift edges, arc terrane edges and mapping choices, not only collision sutures. `named_sutures.csv` lists only five named sutures and no routes, so a true suture test needs a better suture map.
4. **Path length is not flow.** Whether water can travel 100-400 km through rock in a short time is a permeability question I have not touched (O17 and Section 17).
5. **Craton interiors.** About 31% of land (cratons and shields) lies farthest from boundaries and has the thickest, coldest lower crust. If vents follow boundaries, interiors depend on cracks that run far from the mapped lines. That repeats the O16 coverage concern in another form.

### 22.3 Verdict
**Unresolved, consistent but not tested.** Geometry allows boundary-guided venting. Nothing here supports it independently.

- **The heat production inputs are assumed.** They set the reduced heat flow and the answer. A measured xenolith-based Moho temperature (the 2021 Siberian and Tanzanian study, abstract only seen) would replace this computation.

## 25. Phase B step 10: the craton selection problem tested (2026-11-24, FIRST PASS)

**Note (Section 36):** the Moho temperatures and coverage shares here come from a steady-state geotherm built on present surface heat flow, and use present-day crust as a stand-in for pre-Flood Pangea. **Question (from Section 24.8).** A craton can hold a serpentinite layer at the crust base only if its Moho is below the antigorite limit L, and can trigger it only if the heat to be added (L minus Moho temperature) is small. At 26 mW/m² (Siberian-type) all of it holds but under 1% is in the window.

### 25.3 What it shows
1. **The window exists but is narrow.** About 14-47% of the Archean heat-flow range falls in it, depending on the limit and on how much heat can be added. Only a few mW/m² separates "too cold to trigger" from "too hot to keep the layer".
2. **The answer depends on which limit applies.** With the lower, long-run limit (525 °C), a typical Archean craton (41 mW/m²) is mostly above the limit: serpentinite would not have survived there. With the higher, short-run limit (625 °C) a typical craton sits in the window. Over the model's few thousand years the long-run value is the safer one for persistence. This is the least certain input in the section.
3. **The cold end is out.** Cratons at 26-30 mW/m² (Siberian-type) hold the layer but are 150-250 °C below the limit, and the model has no heat source (O20).
4. **A heat source is still required in every case.** Even the best window needs 0-100 °C added at the Moho. The test does not supply it, and the window width (ΔT) is a requirement, not a result.
5. **Circular at one point.** The window assumes a layer exists; Section 21.4 found no evidence that one does under cratons. Passing the temperature test would not show that.

### 25.4 Limits
Median geotherm per craton cell (not per craton); constant conductivity (true temperatures are slightly higher); heat production is a single bulk range for all cratons; the pressure at the Moho (1.0-1.25 GPa) is used only through the cited limits; no real heat-flow data by craton; the Archean heat-flow distribution is unverified.

### 25.5 Verdict
**Narrow but not empty.** A craton-by-craton heat-flow map is needed to say which cratons, if any, fall in the window. As it stands the selection problem makes the serpentinite candidate fragile: it needs the right heat flow, the right limit, an existing layer, and an unidentified heat source at the same time.

## 26. Phase B step 11: the heat carried by vented water (2026-11-24, FIRST PASS, ASSUMED INPUTS)

**Question.** Catastrophic cracks that vent water from the crust base (Sections 17, 21, 22) have never been tested against the heat constraint on released water (H5, Section 3.3: water must arrive at no more than about 159 °C for the present-depth ocean, or 47 °C for a shallow pre-Flood ocean). **Method.** `scripts/vent_water_heat.py`, first-order and with assumed inputs (listed in the script): water volume 1.3×10⁸ km³ (the model's shallow-water target, Section 5.1) or 1.75×10⁸ km³ (from a 3 km serpentinite layer over Pangea's 2.105×10⁸ km², 10.7 wt% water); source temperature 300-580 °C; ocean cap 40 °C; budget B = 1.4×10²⁶ J (heating the 1.335×10⁹ km³ ocean from 15 to 40 °C). ### 26.1a Which water this applies to (correction, 2026-11-24)

### 26.1 Is a vent mechanism necessary?
Partly. It is **necessary for the serpentinite candidate**, where subsidence happens only if the water leaves the layer (Section 21.2), and for **any flood water that comes from depth** ("fountains of the great deep", Genesis 7:11). It is **not needed for the gabbro candidate**, whose subsidence comes from the density change, and not for a flood that takes its water only from shallow sources. Since the serpentinite candidate is the stronger one, the vent problem is close to necessary for the model as it now stands.

### 26.2 Results
| Quantity | Result |
|----------|--------|
| Heat in 1.3×10⁸ km³ of water cooling from 580 °C to 40 °C | 2.9×10²⁶ J = **2.1 times B** |
| Same at 500 °C / 400 °C / 300 °C | 1.8 / 1.4 / 1.0 times B |

### 26.3 Possible solutions (none tested beyond the first-order numbers)
1. **Take less water from depth, more subsidence from the collapse.** The most any source can supply within B is 6.2×10⁷ km³ at 580 °C, 7.3×10⁷ km³ at 500 °C and 9.3×10⁷ km³ at 400 °C (against the 1.3×10⁸ km³ target). The shortfall is made up by extra collapse subsidence at 330 m per 10⁸ km³ (Section 12): about 120 m more at 400 °C, 190 m at 500 °C, 225 m at 580 °C, which raises the collapse from about 0.48 km to 0.6-0.7 km. That needs a 2.5-3 km layer at 23-25% loss, within what Section 21.2 gives. **This is the simplest fix and costs the least.** One consequence: a 2.5-3 km layer holds about 1.5×10⁸ km³ of water (0.7-0.8 km³ per km²), more than can arrive hot at the surface. The collapse needs the water to leave the layer, not to reach the ocean, so the rest would have to move up and stay in the crust above (about 8×10⁷ km³, a water layer of about 0.4 km over Pangea, which the crust's pore and fracture space may or may not hold). That storage is unchecked.
2. **Cool the water in the crust (diffuse flow, not cracks).** The crust can absorb the heat: cooling about 2 km of rock by 250 K over Pangea takes up all of it. Full heat exchange in 150 days needs flow paths no more than about 7 m apart (twice the 3.6 m conduction length). That needs a permeability of about 3.6×10⁻¹³ m² (from the Darcy flux of 6.4×10⁻⁵ m/s and assumed hot-water viscosity), or fractures about 0.3 mm wide every 7 m, against typical crustal permeability of 10⁻¹⁸ to 10⁻¹⁶ m². A crust that fractured is very extreme and contradicts "a few cracks along sutures".
3. **A cooler source.** Water released from lower-temperature reactions (lizardite or brucite breakdown at well under 500 °C, or shallow sources already in Section 5.1) would carry less heat. The reaction temperatures were not checked.
4. **Let the heat go to the atmosphere as steam.** Hot water decompressing to the surface partly boils; the latent heat goes to the air, not the ocean. This could lower the heat reaching the ocean but moves it to the atmosphere. Calculated in Section 27: the heat is moved, not removed.
5. **Raise the heat budget.** B comes from an unverified 40 °C cap (O9). A higher cap helps, but it is a premise to defend, not a solution.
6. **Spread the venting over a longer time.** The Flood's 150 days is fixed by P2, so this is not available.

### 26.4 Verdict
**A real problem, not a fatal one yet.** Crack-vented deep water exceeds the heat budget by about a factor of 2 and cannot be cooled by narrow cracks. The cheapest fix is accepting less deep water and about 0.1-0.2 km more collapse; the fracture-network fix needs an implausibly permeable crust. Status: open, with a candidate fix (solution 1) that needs the collapse to reach 0.6-0.7 km.

### 26.5 Limits
First-order only: constant specific heat (heat is understated); no boiling, decompression cooling or steam; assumed viscosity, rock properties and a single crack spacing taken from province boundaries (Section 22); the 150-day window is applied to cooling; the heat budget and the 40 °C cap are unverified. The Darcy flux uses the 3 km layer's water over 150 days.

### 27.1 Can water be stored as a free layer at the crust base?
**Prompted by** a pasted search summary that assumed a "trapped water layer" at the Moho (unsourced, not read from a paper). `scripts/free_water_layer.py`. All inputs are from memory: fracture toughness 1-3 MPa·m^½, water 1.1 g/cm³ and viscosity 1×10⁻⁴ Pa·s at about 1 GPa, rock 2.8 g/cm³, lower-crust viscosity 10¹⁹-10²³ Pa·s, permeability 10⁻¹⁹-10⁻¹⁵ m², porosity 1-3%.
| Check | Result |
|-------|--------|
| **Brittle crust:** height of a connected water body at which buoyancy alone drives a crack upward | about 15-32 m, so a layer 0.5-0.9 km thick would escape at once |
| **Ductile crust:** time for buoyant water to cross 35 km by slow flow | 10²-10⁶ years; under 1,650 years only if permeability is above about 10⁻¹⁶ m² |
| **Density inversion:** time for the crust to sink into a water layer (crust over lighter water) | 5×10² years at viscosity 10¹⁹ Pa·s; 5×10³ years at 10²⁰; 5×10⁴ years at 10²¹ |

**Reading.** A free layer survives the pre-Flood centuries only in a narrow case: the rock must be ductile (not brittle), the permeability very low (below about 10⁻¹⁷ m², where ascent takes more than 6,000 years), and the lower crust stiff (viscosity of 10²⁰ Pa·s or more). In brittle or well-fractured crust the water escapes by itself. In any case the layer cannot be thick, since the crust above must be held up by the framework, not by water pressure. **Verdict: free-water storage is not supported; hydrous minerals (Section 21) remain the workable store.** The critical height is an order-of-magnitude estimate (a factor of 2 or so).

### 27.2 What happens to the water at the surface (steam route)
`scripts/vent_steam.py`, using IAPWS-95 (the `iapws` package, installed with your approval; valid to 1,000 MPa). Water leaves the Moho at 1,000 MPa (or less), rises 35 km with no loss to the walls (0.08% cooling, Section 26), loses only gravitational potential energy (343 kJ/kg), and arrives at 0.1 MPa. The solver gave numerical warnings at some points; the results are plausible but not cross-checked against a steam table.
| Start (1,000 MPa) | Arrival state | Steam mass fraction | Heat to bring it to 40 °C liquid | Against budget B (1.3×10⁸ km³) |
|-------------------|---------------|---------------------|----------------------------------|--------------------------------|
| 400 °C | 100 °C, wet steam | 0.66 | 1,730 kJ/kg | 1.6 |
| 500 °C | 100 °C, wet steam | 0.81 | 2,086 kJ/kg | 1.9 |
| 580 °C | 100 °C, wet steam | 0.94 | 2,368 kJ/kg | 2.2 |
| 700 °C | 240 °C, superheated steam | 1.00 | 2,788 kJ/kg | 2.6 |

At a start pressure of 300-600 MPa the heat is 1.2-2.4 times B. The constant-specific-heat estimate in Section 26 (2.1 times B at 580 °C) is confirmed within 5%.

### 27.3 What the steam route does
- **Most of the heat leaves as latent heat.** At 580 °C, 94% of the water arrives as steam at 100 °C and about 89% of the heat is latent heat, released when the steam condenses.
- **It does not remove the heat.** The heat stays in the system and is released into the air when the steam condenses. About 3.1×10²⁶ J would warm the atmosphere (5.2×10²¹ J/K) by about 59,000 K if it stayed there, so it cannot stay. It must be radiated to space.
- **Radiating it takes decades.** At today's outgoing flux (240 W/m², 1.2×10¹⁷ W over the globe) it takes about 80 years. A hotter atmosphere would radiate faster, but not within 150 days.
- **Scale.** The steam mass is 1.2×10²⁰ kg, about 10⁴ times the present atmospheric water vapour (1.3×10¹⁶ kg).
- **Net effect for CKDT:** the steam route relieves the ocean, not the budget. **It converts "too much heat in the ocean" into "an atmospheric heat load lasting decades."** Whether the ocean stays at or below 40 °C then depends on how much of the condensation heat reaches the ocean and how much leaves to space.

### 27.4 Verdict
**Open, not solved.** The steam route is real physics and moves most of the heat into the atmosphere, but the total heat is unchanged and exceeds the budget by about 2. The best fix remains the one in Section 26.3 (take less deep water and more collapse subsidence). Status: no mechanism yet shown that gets rid of the heat within the Flood's 150 days.

## 29. Phase E step 1: craton heat flow from the Lucazeau grid (2026-11-25, FIRST PASS)

### 29.2 What it shows
1. **The earlier heat-flow assumption was too low.** The grid's median craton value is 58 mW/m², not 41. At that heat flow most of the craton area (82-92%, depending on L) has a computed Moho already above the antigorite limit, so a serpentinite layer there would be unstable now, not waiting for a trigger.
2. **Only 5-14% of craton area is in the window** where the layer could be stable and need little added heat. This is the selection problem of Section 25 with measured input, and it is no better: K11 (layer exists) and K12 (Moho hot enough to trigger) are not helped.

### 29.3 Limits
- The grid is a **prediction** (generalized similarity method) with its own uncertainty (`sHF_pred`, not used); only 912 of 5,709 cells hold an observation. Predicted values in poorly sampled cratons follow geology, not measurements.
- The Hasterok craton polygons include terranes with higher heat flow than a classic Archean craton core.

- The antigorite limit L is the experiment range in Section 21.2; one-week and long-run values differ by 70-100 °C.
- Moho depth is CRUST1.0 (1° cells).

### 29.4 Verdict
**K11 and K12 stay Unsupported, and the window is narrow: about 5-14% of craton area.** Section 25's conclusion (the serpentinite candidate needs the right heat flow, limit, layer and heat source together) is confirmed, not softened. K18, K9 and the rest of Section 28 are unchanged.

## 30. Phase E step 2: resolving the heat-flow disagreement (2026-11-25, FIRST PASS)

**Note (Section 36):** the Moho temperatures and coverage shares here come from a steady-state geotherm built on present surface heat flow, and use present-day crust as a stand-in for pre-Flood Pangea. **Question.** Section 29 gave a craton median Moho of about 770 °C from the Lucazeau grid, against measured xenolith values of below 400 °C (Siberia) and 500-600 °C (Tanzania, Section 24.6). ### 30.2 Findings
1. **The grid is not the problem.** Where cratons have measurements, the grid matches them (difference 0.3 mW/m²). The Section 29 median of 58 mW/m² is what the data say for the polygons, within the sampling limits below.
2. **The xenolith sites are consistent with the model.** Udachnaya's measured 15-22 mW/m² gives a Moho well under 400 °C (Section 24.7: about 250 °C at 19 mW/m²). The Tanzanian craton's 35 mW/m² (grid 39) is within a few mW/m² of the 38 mW/m² that gives 500 °C, so it is consistent with the measured 500-600 °C within the model's spread. Local heat flow and xenolith temperature agree at both sites.
3. **The disagreement is one of representativeness.** The two xenolith sites are the cold end of the craton set. The median craton in the 114 polygons has about 52 mW/m², which on this geotherm is a Moho near 650-700 °C, and the xenolith values (two craton sites, plus the Mozambique Belt above 650 °C) do not sample that. The "median craton Moho of 500 °C" used in Sections 24-25 was too low for the craton set as a whole.
4. **The heat-production assumption matters less than expected.** Going from 0.3-0.6 to 0.8-1.0 µW/m³ lowers the median Moho by 47 °C (699 to 652 °C). It does not close the gap.
5. **The conductive geotherm is not valid at the hot end.** A 90th percentile of 1000 °C and higher is unphysical for a stable craton (it would mean melting or advection), so the hot tail is hot spots, volcanic or hydrothermal sites, or polygons that are not stable craton. Only about 40-45% of cratons by count have a median Moho at or below 625 °C.

### 30.3 What this does to the collapse candidates
- **Serpentinite:** the share of craton area in the window is small (Section 29: 5-14%) and the craton set is hotter than the Tanzanian and Siberian cases that Sections 24 and 25 used. Roughly 20-29% of cratons (by count) have a Moho at or below the 525 °C antigorite limit, and most of those are near it. Cratons that are hotter than the limit would already have lost their serpentinite (K11, K12).
- **Heat to add:** at the cold end (Siberian type) the added heat is 100-200 °C or more; at the hot end it is zero or negative. Section 24.7's range stands, but the middle of the craton set is hotter than its "central" values.
- **K12 stays Unsupported.** It is more accurate to say the Moho temperature varies by craton from under 300 to over 800 °C, and the model must name which craton it is describing. No single craton type passes the serpentinite test without also failing another (layer existence, trigger).

### 30.4 Limits
- Points are clustered: 36 cratons have none, and many points sit in geothermal, volcanic or basin sites (the 90th percentile of 200 mW/m² shows this). Per-craton medians reduce but do not remove this bias.
- The Hasterok craton polygons include terranes that are not stable Archean cores.
- Moho depth is a single 39 km (the CRUST1.0 median); the geotherm parameters are assumed (decay depth, conductivity, constant over depth).
- Measured heat production in the NGHF is from surface samples and cannot be used as the bulk crustal value.
- No per-craton named list was produced.

### 30.5 Verdict
**Resolved: the grid and the xenolith data agree where they overlap, and the model's earlier "central" craton Moho (500 °C) was too cool for the craton set as a whole.** The craton Moho is a spread, not a point: cold at Siberian-type cratons, near the antigorite limit at Tanzanian-type ones, and above it for most others. This narrows the serpentinite candidate further and does not change any Section 28 verdict.

### 31.4 Verdict

## 33. Phase E step 5: are there lower-crustal earthquakes in cratons? (2026-11-25, FIRST PASS, SEARCH ONLY)

### 33.1 Findings

### 33.2 What it shows
1. **The precondition is real.** Brittle failure in the lower crust at 30-40 km does occur in cratonic Africa. A lower-crustal earthquake at a craton Moho is therefore not excluded by observation.
2. **Today's quietness is not evidence against a Flood-time cascade.** Cratons are on the whole quiet now, and the deep events cited sit in rifts and at lithosphere-thickness steps. That describes the present. The model says today is the state after the collapse, with the stress released, so it predicts quiet cratons. The modern pattern therefore cannot test the trigger either way. What the observation does show is that the physics is available (brittle failure at 30-40 km). What it cannot show is whether the stress and trigger existed at the time of the Flood. Any such stress source would have to be named as an assumption (P4) and cannot be checked from today's seismicity.
3. **The same papers say the lower crust is dry.** A strong, dry granulite is what lets it be brittle, and it is also why the reaction needs fluid (O17). The observation supports the trigger and the fluid problem at once.
4. **Nothing found links an earthquake to a reaction in a craton.** The only linked case is the Holsnøy (Norway) analog already in Section 17.

### 33.3 Limits
Two searches, excerpts only, no focal-depth catalogue, and no study specific to Kaapvaal, Slave or Canadian Shield interiors. The papers' age statements are not used (P1).

### 33.4 Verdict
**K15 stays Unresolved, with a small gain:** lower-crustal rupture at craton Moho depth is observed, so the physics is available. Today's seismicity cannot test whether it happened at the Flood, and nothing ties an earthquake to a reaction.

### 33.5 Follow-up
- Look for evidence a past cascade would leave in cratons today, since seismicity cannot show it: reaction fronts or pseudotachylyte (fault-melt) in lower-crustal rock at Moho depth, a dense or high-velocity layer at the base of cratonic crust, and fractures along province boundaries (Section 22).
- Focal-depth catalogue studies of the Kaapvaal, Slave, Tanzania and Canadian Shield interiors would still show how brittle the lower crust is now.

### 34.8 Addendum: the garnet-granulite boundary search (2026-11-25, one extended search, abstracts only)
**Timeline correction (Section 36.3):** the "unreacted today" condition below should read unreacted before the Flood and reacted today where the collapse acted.
- **The transition is wide and gradual, not a line.** An experimental study of the gabbro-eclogite transformation (*Tectonophysics* 1966, authors not shown in the excerpt) puts the garnet-granulite transitional zone at about 3.4 to 12 kbar (0.34-1.2 GPa) at 1,100 °C. The pressure at which garnet appears and plagioclase disappears depends on the bulk composition. Density and seismic velocity change gradually across it.
- **Eclogite** is commonly described as forming above about 500 °C and 1.2 GPa for natural rocks (*Scientific Reports* excerpt). Mineral-equilibria modelling shows the granulite-eclogite transition is sensitive to ferric iron and bulk composition.
- **What this does to mode G:**
  1. The assumed boundary (T of 600 °C or more, P of 1.0 GPa or more) is not unreasonable, and may be conservative: the transition at lower temperature is expected at lower pressure than at 1,100 °C (slope not checked).
  2. **A gradual transition weakens the "unreacted today" condition.** If hot lower crust at 1.0-1.2 GPa and 700-900 °C is inside the transition zone, then at equilibrium it is already partly garnet granulite, and the collapse would be only the part that is kinetically stuck. Dry granulite that stays out of equilibrium is documented (Holsnøy), but how much of the 10% is still unreacted is not known.
  3. **The 10% density gain is a maximum,** reached only after full conversion. Partial conversion lowers the supply by the same share.
- **Status:** the boundary is still not sourced as a curve; this is bracketing only. Mode G stays conditional, now with a stated risk that the hot lower crust is already (partly) converted. Section 34.6 stands, and the "unreacted today" test in 34.7 matters more.

### 34.9 Addendum: is the lower crust unreacted today? (2026-11-25, one search, abstracts only; INCONCLUSIVE)
- **Siberia:** the lower crust is described as mainly mafic granulite, possibly heterogeneous. Very high P-wave velocities (above 7.2 km/s) in the lowermost crust are fitted by a mixture of rock types, and the same work argues for multistage magma underplating at the crust-mantle boundary.
- **Kaapvaal:** one paper notes that cratonic lower crust may be less mafic than lower crust under Proterozoic terranes (citing earlier work). That would lower the mafic fraction f for cratons.
- **Xenoliths are not faithful samples of in-situ velocity or density** (Hannuoba study): entrainment and surfacing change them. They cannot settle whether the rock is gabbro or garnet granulite at depth.
- **Not found:** Tanzania lower-crust data, density values for gabbro and garnet granulite, or a clean link between a 7.0-7.4 km/s layer and either rock.
- **Reading:** a mafic, heterogeneous lower crust with very high velocities at the base fits some garnet-bearing material already being present at the base, which is the risk named in 34.8. It is not shown either way. The craton mafic fraction may be below 0.5, and Section 34.2 shows G falls to 0.6% (0.48 km) or 29% (0.28 km) of continental area at f = 0.3.
- **Status:** the "unreacted today" test cannot be run with the sources found. Mode G stays conditional.

## 35. Phase E step 7: the net sea-level effect of the shallow water (O9) (2026-11-25, FIRST PASS, CORRECTS SECTION 5.1)

**Question.** Section 34's route rests on 1.3×10⁸ km³ of released shallow water (Section 5.1). Are those reservoirs sourced, and does each cubic kilometre of water leaving rock raise sea level by 196 m per 10⁸ km³ as the budget assumes? **Method.** One web search (sediment and oceanic-crust water) and `scripts/water_net_effect.py`. The calculation is arithmetic on volumes; no new physical input beyond the search values.

### 35.1 The error in the budget
The Section 4.2 and 16 water budget holds **the ocean floor fixed**. That is correct only for water whose source space does not become ocean basin. Water that leaves rock **under the ocean floor** lets the floor settle: the sediment compacts or the rock shrinks, and the basin gains volume equal to the volume lost. Only the difference raises sea level.
| Reservoir | Gross (km³) | Net effect on sea level | Net (km³) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Continental groundwater, to 2 km (Gleeson et al. 2016, sourced) | 2.26×10⁷ | the same as gross: the land does not become basin | 2.26×10⁷ |
| Ocean-sediment pore water (model 6×10⁷; **LaRowe et al. 2017: 8.46×10⁷**, sourced) | 6×10⁷ to 8.46×10⁷ | **zero**: the sediment compacts as the water leaves and the basin gains exactly that volume (if the pores stayed open the ocean would refill them) | 0 |
| Oceanic serpentinite (model: 5×10⁷, assumed) | 5×10⁷ | (w − s) / w, where w is the water volume per unit rock volume (0.28 at 10.7 wt%, 0.34 at 13 wt%) and s the solid shrink (0.23-0.25): **0.10-0.32** | 0.5-1.6×10⁷ |
| **Total** | **1.33×10⁸** (model) | | **2.8-3.9×10⁷** |

### 35.2 What it does to the flood geometry
Pre-Flood heights Beta(1.5, 3) on 0-610 m, mean about 200 m (same as Section 34.3):
| Water counted | V_r (km³) | Share of Pangea that must be lowered | Largest drop | Uniform lowering (Section 16 case A) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Gross model total (Section 5.1) | 1.33×10⁸ | 17% | 0.27 km | 0.47 km |
| **Net, upper** | 3.9×10⁷ | **74%** | **0.50 km** | 0.78 km |
| **Net, lower** | 2.8×10⁷ | **83%** | **0.53 km** | 0.82 km |

Coverage by the two collapse modes of Section 34 for a drop of 0.52 km at the highest ground: **28%** of continental area (mafic fraction 0.5, `coverage_modes.py`) and 3% at a mafic fraction of 0.3 (33% of continental area at best, with mafic fraction 1.0).

### 35.3 What it does to the model
1. **The 1.3×10⁸ km³ is a gross volume, not a sea-level source.** About half of it (the sediment pore water) is worth nothing, and the serpentinite share is worth 10-32%. The net is about **3×10⁷ km³**, close to the groundwater alone.
2. **Route 1 of Section 34 closes.** With 1.3×10⁸ km³ only 17% of land needed lowering. With the net water, 74-83% of land does, by up to 0.5 km, so the highest-ground shortcut mostly disappears.
3. **Section 16, 26 and 34 numbers that used 1.3×10⁸ km³ need re-reading:** the 0.48 km "uniform with all shallow water" case becomes about 0.8 km; cases B and C of Section 26.1a (Moho water at the cap plus sediment pore water, or shallow reservoirs only) lose most of their water.
4. **Not affected:** water that comes out of the continental Moho layer itself. It is under land, so its full volume counts. That is a smaller supply than it looks (the window is about 2% of Pangea), and it brings the heat problem back (O21).
5. **The sediment figure is now sourced** (LaRowe et al. 2017 gives 8.46×10⁷ km³, about 6.3% of sediment volume), which turns an assumption into a verified number. It does not help the budget.

### 35.4 Limits
- The cancellation assumes the sediment and rock settle freely; a rigid, open pore space under the ocean would be filled by ocean water, which gives the same net of zero.
- Net fractions for serpentinite use the Section 21 shrink (23-25%) and assumed water contents; the 5×10⁷ km³ reservoir size is itself an assumption. No global total for water in oceanic crust was found.
- The pre-Flood height distribution is invented (Section 34.5).
- The sea floor settling may also change other things not modelled (isostatic response of the displaced water load).
- One web search; the LaRowe paper itself was not opened.

### 35.5 Verdict
**Unsupported in the form used, and the correction goes against the model.** The shallow-water supply is about 3×10⁷ km³, not 1.3×10⁸. K7 is Contradicted as a net sea-level source (K7 row updated). The collapse must lower most of Pangea by up to 0.5 km (uniform 0.8 km) unless a water source that is not under the ocean floor is found. Mode G and the serpentinite window together reach 28% of continental area at best for 0.52 km, so coverage is a larger problem than Section 34 reported.

### 35.6 Follow-up
- Look for water sources whose space is not ocean basin: deeper continental pore and fracture water, hydrous minerals in continental crust, the sub-continental mantle wedge, or the Moho water of Section 26 (each raises the heat question).
- Check whether the model could instead raise sea level by displacing water (basins that fill, rising floor) rather than adding it; this was not examined.
- Open and read LaRowe et al. 2017; look for a global water inventory of the oceanic crust.
- Re-run Sections 16, 26.1a and 34 with net water (the key figures are in 35.2).

### 36.2 A conflict in the method
Sections 24, 25 and 29-31 infer the Moho temperature from the surface heat flow by assuming a **steady conduction profile**. Section 3.2 gives the conduction time through 10 km as about 3 million years and through 100 km as about 300 million. Under P1 (about 6,000 years) a steady profile cannot have formed, and a surface heat flow constrains only the top few hundred metres (√(κt) for κ = 10⁻⁶ m²/s and 4,370 years is about 0.37 km). The thermal state at depth is then an initial condition, not something surface heat flow measures. The H3 argument (heat released at 35 km stays deep for about 40 million years) uses the same slow conduction, so the two parts of the model disagree.
- **Consequence.** Only direct measurements (the xenolith temperatures of Section 24.6) can be used for Moho temperature. The heat-flow maps stay as a picture of what a steady-state Earth would show.
- **DTAT.** DTAT Section 12 lists "the Moho temperature of cratons (CKDT 24-25)" as an item handed to it. That item carries the same limit.

### 36.3 A correction to the timeline in 34.8-34.9
Those addenda asked whether the hot lower crust is **unreacted today**. Under the model's timeline it must be unreacted **before the Flood** and already reacted **today** in places where the collapse acted. The fingerprint to look for is therefore a dense lower crust (garnet granulite, 3.2-3.3 g/cm³, Section 19.2) in the collapsed places.
- **Check made (`scripts/coverage_modes.py`):** CRUST1.0 lower-crust density is 2.92 g/cm³ (10-90%: 2.86-3.03) over continental crust, **2.92 in the G candidate cells** and 2.91 in the S window cells, with **0.0% at 3.15 or more** anywhere.
- **Reading:** if mode G had acted over the cells that meet its conditions, a dense lower crust would be expected there. It is not seen, but CRUST1.0 densities come from assumed velocity-density relations and are almost uniform, so **the test cannot discriminate**. It is a weak point against G, not a contradiction.

### 36.5 What this does to the model
1. **The map-based "hurts" become "unknown", not "shown to hurt".** The 2% coverage, the 12% craton window and the 84% above the limit were computed from a present steady geotherm. Treat them as the present-day picture only.
2. **K12 rests on Section 24.6 only** (measured xenolith temperatures); Sections 29-31 are not evidence for or against it.
3. **What was not touched:** the heat physics, the reaction physics, the net-water correction (35), and the flood geometry. Section 35, the largest setback, stands.
4. **The model now has a requirements table (36.4) with 12 rows.** Of these, only C2 is consistent with data; two are partly shown (G1, G2 pressure); the rest are not shown. None is contradicted by a direct measurement.

### 36.6 Limits

- The xenolith temperatures are the only direct temperature data; they are three regions plus the Mozambique Belt, and the source was an abstract (Section 24.6).

### 37.1 Pending decision: the thermal state at depth (blocks steps 2-3)
